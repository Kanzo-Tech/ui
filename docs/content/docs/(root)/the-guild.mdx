Every example on this site is about the same thing.

Five guild halls take contracts from one board. The board says what needs doing, where, how bad
it is and what it pays; the roster says who can be sent. That is the whole subject matter, and
once you have read this page you never have to read it again — the table on the data-table page,
the chart on the analytics page and the nodes in the graph showcase are all this.

<Callout title="Why bother">
Before this, the examples each invented their own subject: a select offered "My Dataset", a chart
plotted `requests` against `latency (ms)`, a table listed pipelines and a graph held RDF triples.
Every one was reasonable alone. Together they made the reader re-learn the domain on every page,
and they never let two components be seen agreeing about anything — which is most of what a design
system has to prove.
</Callout>

## The halls

A hall is this world's **tenant**, and the reason the world is a guild rather than a bakery: a hall has colours, and colours are the thing this library themes.

`heraldry` is not decoration. The two hexes are a brand and a neutral, which is where a theme author starts — so "the Order of Salt's colours" and "that tenant's theme" are the same sentence. Switching halls is how the white-label story gets
told, with no fixture that exists only to demonstrate theming.

## The people

Thirty-five members across the five halls. Six roles, each with a duty a rule can actually
require — which is why the rule language can say *a party this size must carry a cantor* and mean
something.

<RolesTable />

Availability is **derived from the board**, never authored beside it. The first version of this
world authored both and they disagreed within the hour: the generator staffed a member marked
`missing` onto a contract that had just been signed. Now being *out* is computed from who is on a
live contract, and only the narrative exceptions — resting, wounded, missing — are written down.

<Callout type="warn" title="Both can be true">
Dagfinn Roe is wounded *and* afield, on a grade-5 contract that is six days overdue with a party of
two and no cantor. The condition wins the label, because that is the one a quartermaster needs to
see. That contract is also the one the rule examples reject — a real violation rather than a
hypothetical one.
</Callout>

## The archive

The board is what is happening. The archive is what has happened — every contract the halls have
closed, going back about eight years.

It exists because of the graph. A network view is only worth a WebGL canvas when things *share*:
the same member turns up on contract after contract, the same beast in region after region, so
lassoing a cluster pulls in work you did not know was related. Forty-four contracts cannot do
that.

But it is history rather than padding, and this is the part worth knowing if you ever edit the
world. Each member's **settled** count on the roster above used to be a number with no cause.
Parties in the archive are drawn against that count as a quota, until the quota is spent — so the
archive *is* the history those numbers were counting, and a test fails if the two ever stop
agreeing.

## The board

Forty-four contracts. Five pages at ten a page, so pagination is doing something and the last page
is not a stub.

The derived half — ids, rewards, dates, parties — comes from one seeded stream, so this is the same
board in every browser and every build. Nothing in the world reads the clock: today is
**14 September 1312**, and a test fails if any fixture calls `Date.now()`. A reader who sees `1312`
in a date field knows immediately that it is the example world talking and not their own data.
