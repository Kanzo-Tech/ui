[The graph page](/docs/graph) is what `@kanzo-tech/graph` ships. This page is how it is built and
why — and every rule here was decided by a number or by a reference we can fetch, because at this
size taste is not available.

<Callout type="info">
**This page led the code, and the code has caught up with all of it but three parts.** The core,
the renderer, the adapter and `GraphCanvas` exist and are held by the tests each rule cites.
`GraphLegend`, `GraphToolbar` and `GraphInspector` still live in the workspace showcase, so the
parts rule and the acceptance test still lead, and say what will hold them.
</Callout>

## One path: fossil is the backend, this package is the view

fossil reads the corpus and this package draws it, and nothing crosses between them except the
corpus contract as fossil wrote it — the contract section of fossil's own design page, *The
backend*: `open`, `tileMatrix`, `scan(…).plan()`, `scan(…).read(address)`, `edges`, `node`
and `types`. **No SQL is written in `@kanzo-tech/graph`.** Every question the view asks is a call on the
corpus, and a second way to reach the same bytes is deleted rather than kept beside the first.

```text
fossil (backend)                               @kanzo-tech/graph (view)
open(url, { engine })  → Corpus                    parts     GraphCanvas · GraphLegend · GraphToolbar · GraphInspector
tileMatrix(type) → matrices, tiles, boxes          adapter   useGraph · GraphRoot · GraphRootProvider · useGraphContext
scan({ type, filter, select }).plan() → tasks      render    batches → cosmos.gl buffers, one render per frame
scan.read({ type, z, tile }, { signal }) → Batch   core      tileset: cull · select · refine · cache · cancel
edges({ from, direction, signal }) → batches, gaps filter    the page's crossfilter → scan's typed filter
```

What that deleted was not a style. `duck-source.ts` was 1,096 lines, and it was this package writing
fossil's half a second time: it composed tile URLs from fossil's addressing, read Parquet footers
for boxes, built the visible, anchor and stride CTEs, joined every edge relation and counted the
window — and fossil's own reader answers the same questions from the manifest it wrote. Of the ten
costs measured against it on 2026-09-29 (the table is under
[*What it closes*](#what-it-closes-and-who-closes-it)), six are that duplication and four are this
side not keeping what it had already read.

**What would reverse it:** a question the view has to ask that the corpus contract cannot express
and fossil declines to add. That is a gap in the contract, raised on fossil's side — never a licence
to write the query here, because the query written here is how there came to be two readers.

**The one value the view takes from fossil is its role table.** `PAYLOAD_ADDRESS` and
`PAYLOAD_COORDINATES` are what `@fossil-lang/corpus` exports so that a reader asks for the column it
means rather than spelling the writer's names again; they are the only values imported from it.

*Held by* `packages/graph/src/layers.test.ts`, "writes no SQL, and reaches fossil only through its
types and its role table"; `packages/graph/src/index.test.ts`, "keeps the second reader deleted: no
opener, no source, no slice", `!openCorpus`, `!DuckSource`, `!BoundedSource`.

## ADR 0001 — the canvas stops holding the graph, and asks instead

<Callout type="info">
This one keeps its number. fossil cites it from the other side of the seam, so the label has to
survive; the sentence beside it says what it decided, and there is nothing to go and read.
</Callout>

The canvas is a **client of a bounded source**, and the path that held everything is deleted. A
headless tileset owns the asking: it observes the camera, selects the tiles in view, cancels what the
camera has superseded, and hands what arrived to the renderer. `load()` and `Loaded` were deleted,
not deprecated. Detail is **fetched, not carried** — a tile is geometry, and a hover asks for one
node's detail. And **identity is an id, never an index**: buffer positions do not survive a change of
tiles, so selection, focus and pinned sets are held as `VertexId`s and re-resolved against each
composition, and a selection's *size* comes from the query rather than from what is drawn.

The measurement that forced it: holding the whole corpus cost **1,225 ms of first paint at 200,000
nodes** on an M4 Pro and stopped being viable short of a million. Asking for what is visible, capped,
cost **105 ms at the same size and 30 ms per camera move**, and its first paint stops scaling with the
corpus — four times the nodes costs 36% more rather than 400%.

**One premise underpins all of it: the graph is not edited.** It is a derived artefact — fossil is a
compiler and the graph is its output, so a change comes from editing the mapping or the source data
and recompiling, never from writing to the graph. That premise is what makes immutable Parquet the
right shape, a tile cache that never invalidates on its own correct, and a render path designed
entirely around reading not missing its other half.

The costs were weighed and are real. A hover card that used to be instantaneous now waits. The label
budget loses its global ordering — *the most important nodes here* is arguably the better question,
but it is a different answer.

**Three things implementing it taught, and each survives the move to tiles:**

- **A corpus's coordinate space *is* the camera's.** A rescale between the two means the index and
  the viewport describe different places. It failed exactly that way when positions were normalised
  on the way in.
- **"Ask once" has to be enforced where the asking is.** A graph that fitted was answered whole and
  then re-asked about whatever rectangle the reader had zoomed to, replacing a complete answer with an
  empty one. In a tileset this is structural: a tile already loaded is never asked again.
- **The canvas element cannot be gated on the first answer.** Rendered behind a placeholder it is a
  deadlock: no element, no renderer, no camera, no question, no answer. Waiting is an overlay over an
  empty canvas.

**What would reverse it:** a corpus small enough, and a host common enough, that holding it costs less
than the machinery of asking. The measurement says the crossover is below the smallest corpus fossil
writes for this canvas.

*Held by* `packages/graph/src/core/resident.test.ts`, "re-resolves a vertex that left the answer and
came back at a different position"; `packages/graph/src/index.test.ts`, "ships a canvas that owns
the renderer, and neither load nor Loaded".

## The layers, and the reference each one copies

The package is four layers and a bridge, and **each has a named reference whose shape it takes**.
That is the rule, not a description of it: a layer with no reference is a black box, and a black box
here is how one module reached a thousand lines.

| layer | reference | owns | owns nothing of |
|---|---|---|---|
| **core** — the tileset | deck.gl `Tileset2D` | which tiles, in what order, what is kept, what is cancelled | React, the DOM, cosmos.gl, SQL |
| **render** | cosmos.gl's own buffer API | batches → typed arrays, one upload per changed kind, one render per frame | when to ask, what the tiles are |
| **adapter** | TanStack Query's core/adapter split; Ark's root/provider/context | the store's lifetime in React, and the context the parts read | how a tile loads |
| **parts** | Ark's flat parts over one context | the canvas element, legend, toolbar, inspector | any product's policy |
| **filter** — the bridge | Mosaic's `Selection` | turning the page's crossfilter into scan's typed `filter`, and publishing the graph's own clause | the coordinator's queries |

The modules, each well under five hundred lines — the budget is the point of splitting them, and it
is enforceable:

```text
packages/graph/src/
├── core/                     no React; nothing here imports cosmos.gl
│   ├── tile-matrix.ts        viewport + tileMatrix → the tiles in view and the level (getTileIndices)
│   ├── tile.ts               one tile: load, abort, content, byteLength, parent/children (Tile2DHeader)
│   ├── tileset.ts            update(viewport) → selected; tree; cache; prune          (Tileset2D)
│   ├── refine.ts             best-available, and only it                              (STRATEGY_DEFAULT)
│   ├── scheduler.ts          priority queue, maxRequests, debounce, cancel by priority (RequestScheduler)

├── render/
│   ├── encode.ts             one tile's Batch → its typed arrays, once per tile per channel binding
│   ├── compose.ts            visible tiles → one buffer set; the link join
│   ├── renderer.ts           cosmos.gl's lifetime, dirty flags by kind, and the frame that renders once
│   └── graph-looks · graph-model · graph-sim · obligations · css-color · adaptive · when-ready
```

`GraphLegend`, `GraphToolbar` and `GraphInspector` join `parts/` when they leave the workspace —
[the parts rule](#the-parts-own-the-vocabulary-the-host-owns-the-policy). `cluster-ring.ts` is gone
with the `clusters` prop it served: one group per buffer position means nothing once the buffers are
a composition of tiles, and no host passed one.

**What would reverse it:** a layer whose reference stops describing what it does — the tileset
growing a behaviour `Tileset2D` has no counterpart for, say — at which point either the reference is
wrong for us and the page names a better one, or the behaviour is in the wrong layer.

*Held by* `packages/graph/src/layers.test.ts`, "keeps every module under four hundred lines", "keeps
the core free of React and of cosmos.gl".

## The core is a tileset, shaped as deck.gl's `Tileset2D`

The query loop — a React hook that debounced the camera, asked one rectangle and replaced the whole
answer — becomes a **headless tileset**. It is deck.gl's `Tileset2D`
(`modules/geo-layers/src/tileset-2d/tileset-2d.ts`) with the loader swapped for the corpus, and the
parts that are copied are copied by name, so a reader who knows deck.gl can read this module and one
who does not can read deck.gl's.

**Taken verbatim:**

- **The load.** Loading a tile is deck.gl's `getTileData({ index, id, bbox, signal })` — here,
  `scan.read(address, { signal })` and the tile's edges, with the `AbortSignal` owned by the tile's
  own `AbortController` exactly as `Tile2DHeader._loadData` owns it. A result whose loader id is no
  longer the tile's is discarded as stale. The signal reaches the running statement: fossil's
  `Engine.query` takes it and interrupts the query, not only the queue in front of it.
- **The tile's states.** `isLoaded`, `isLoading`, `needsReload`, `isSelected`, `isVisible`, `parent`,
  `children`, `content`, `byteLength` — the same flags, meaning the same things.
- **`update(viewport)`.** Selection is recomputed when the viewport changed; then tile states, then
  request pruning, then the cache resize, in deck.gl's order.
- **Refinement, under its default only.** `'best-available'`: a selected tile that has not arrived is
  covered by its nearest loaded ancestor, and failing that by its loaded descendants — which is how a
  pan never shows a hole. It is the only strategy, and there is no prop to choose another: a parent
  cell tile is drawn **only while its children load, never at rest**. That is the owner's call of
  2026-09-29, and it is what `'best-available'` already does, since a tile at rest has no unloaded
  selected descendant to stand in for.
- **Scheduling.** A queued request whose tile is neither selected nor visible gets priority `-1` and
  is cancelled before it is issued; the rest are issued nearest-the-centre first. That is
  `Tileset2D._getRequestPriority` over loaders.gl's `RequestScheduler`.
- **The cache.** `maxCacheSize` defaults to five times the selected count (`DEFAULT_CACHE_SCALE`),
  `maxCacheByteSize` is optional, and eviction removes tiles that are neither visible nor selected, in
  insertion order. That order is deck.gl's, and it is not LRU; it is copied knowingly.
- **A change of data keeps the old picture.** When the question changes — a filter, a channel — the
  tiles are marked for reload and keep their content until the new answer arrives, which is
  `reloadAll()` and what `TileLayer` does when its `data` prop moves.

**Where it departs, and why:**

| deck.gl | here | why |
|---|---|---|
| a tile is `{ x, y, z }`, id `` `${x}-${y}-${z}` `` | a tile is fossil's `TileAddress`, `{ type, z, tile }`, id `` `${type}-${z}-${tile}` `` | `dense_id` is a vertex's **rank** in the Morton order, so a matrix is one-dimensional: a tile is a contiguous id range, not a cell of a grid |
| `getParentIndex`: `{ x/2, y/2, z − 1 }` | the parent of `(z, t)` is `(z − 1, t >> (shift(z − 1) − shift(z)))` | a tree on the rank axis, from the `shift` each matrix publishes: four children per tile between rungs, and `2^B` — sixteen at sixteen vertices a cell — between the payload and the first rung |
| `getOSMTileIndices` or `getIdentityTileIndices`: indices from the viewport by arithmetic | **a cull**: a tile is in view when its published `bbox` meets the viewport | where a rank falls on the plane depends on the positions, so no arithmetic over the matrix can locate it; `tileMatrix(type)` lists every tile with its box, synchronously, and no tile is empty |
| `z` from the viewport's zoom plus `zoomOffset`, tiles of `tileSize` pixels | `z` is the **finest level whose tiles in view hold, by their published `rows`, at most `limit` marks** | the budget is marks rather than pixels, and the counts are the manifest's — see the next rule |
| `maxRequests: 6` | **one** read in flight, the queue ordered by priority | six is the browser's connections per HTTP/1.1 origin; our reader is DuckDB-WASM, one worker behind one port, where a second concurrent request queues rather than overlaps — measured, see [the filter rule](#a-filter-is-a-predicate-not-a-mask) |
| in-flight tiles aborted only while `ongoingRequestCount > maxRequests` | an in-flight tile that stops being selected or visible is **always** aborted | deck.gl keeps a finished request because a parallel slot costs nothing; here the one slot is the only one, and a superseded read holds it |
| `refinementStrategy`: three values and a function | `'best-available'`, fixed | a coarse tile is a stand-in while its children load, never a resting state |
| `renderSubLayers` per tile, a layer per tile | tiles are **composed into one buffer set** for one cosmos.gl instance | cosmos.gl has one set of buffers per graph, not layers; see [the renderer rule](#the-renderer-uploads-what-changed-and-renders-once-a-frame) |
| `zRange`, `modelMatrix` | none | a laid-out graph is flat and unrotated; nothing asks for either |
| a cancelled reload sets `content` to what it read, which is nothing | content outlives a cancelled or failed reload | a change of question keeps the old picture until the new answer arrives, and a pan that supersedes the reload must not blank it |

**The loop's defects that this closes.** Every pan re-asked the full rectangle and replaced the whole
answer, so a pan of one pixel re-read every tile under the window (inefficiency 6). A superseded
question was aborted only at the level of the one request object, and a question that had not been
issued yet had nothing to abort (7). A tile is now the unit of asking, of keeping and of cancelling.

**What would reverse it:** the corpus answering a whole window faster than the tiles that cover it —
measured as first paint and as the median pan over the same corpus and the same camera path. That is
not expected: the window read is the sum of the tile reads plus the join that combined them.

*Held by* `packages/graph/src/core/tileset.test.ts`, against a stub corpus: "reads a selected tile
once, and never asks a loaded tile again", "aborts an in-flight tile that stops being selected or
visible", "never issues a queued tile nobody wants any more", "never evicts a visible tile, and evicts
the rest in insertion order", "draws a parent only while a selected child is loading", "keeps the old
content when the question changes, until the new answer arrives".

## A far view is a coarser level of the pyramid

The level is chosen by arithmetic nobody has to pick: **the finest `z` whose tiles in view hold, by
the manifest's own `rows`, at most `limit` marks.** The matrices run coarsest first, `z = 0` a single
cell and `z = Z` every row. At `Z` a mark is a vertex, and a tile there holds at most `tileRows` —
4,096 — so any tile can be drawn as vertices under the default budget of 20,000. Below `Z` a mark is
a **cell**: a summary the writer computed, drawn at **the centroid of its members** and sized by its
exact `count`. There is no sample, no stride and no zoom threshold.

This replaces *a far view is a sample*: a window over the limit used to come back as one row in every
`ceil(matched / limit)` of the corpus' Morton-ordered `dense_id`. The stride was the right answer for
a reader with no pyramid, and it said so itself: *a precomputed pyramid is still the better answer and
is writer work*. The writer has done it, the level files the stride stood in for are retired on
fossil's side, and the stride's SQL leaves with the rest.

**The metric stays, because without one this is taste.** Zoomed all the way out a reader cannot
perceive vertices: at five million the corpus falls into 960,000 pixels and 84.6% of its mass is in
pixels holding eight or more. What is left perceptible is *where the mass is*. So a far view means
something when it reproduces the mass field at the resolution it is looked at — the normalised L1 of
the mass field against the truth at screen resolution, in blocks of eight pixels. The **null** is a
uniform field over the bounding box; the **ceiling** is a density mipmap. **A summary is admissible
when its L1@8px is at most half the null** — at five million, 0.366.

| candidate | marks at 5M | L1@8px at 200k · 1M · 5M |
|---|---|---|
| one point per `community` | 8 | 0.998 · 1.000 · 0.999 |
| one point per `cluster_id` | 15,310 | 0.932 · 0.989 · 0.985 |
| *uniform null* | — | *1.044 · 0.829 · 0.731* |
| `cluster_id` as the box of its members | 15,310 | 1.018 · 0.737 · 0.591 |
| random sample of 20,000 | 20,000 | 0.349 · 0.382 · 0.387 |
| stride sample of 20,000 — what shipped until this page | 20,000 | 0.167 · 0.240 · 0.269 |
| **density mipmap — what a cells level is** | *342,816* | **0.161 · 0.159 · 0.139** |

Read the last row against the one above it. A cells level *is* the density mipmap, cut to the level
whose marks fit the budget — so the ceiling the stride was scored against is now what draws. And the
first two rows are why a cell is **spatial**: a summary keyed by a category column scored worse than a
uniform grey box, and nothing here groups by a column.

**A cell summarises one channel and its membership, not the row**, and the channels mean less on it.
Its columns are the writer's: `x` and `y` (the member centroid), `count`, `internal` — the weight of the
edges absorbed inside it — and `mode` and `purity`, the majority value of the one categorical the
manifest names as its `mode_channel`, and the share of members holding it. So on a cell, **`fill` is
`mode`** when `fill` binds that channel and a single colour otherwise, **`r` can only mean `count`**,
and `symbol` follows `fill`. A host binding `fill` to another column gets its colour back at `Z` and
nowhere above.

**A filter on a column the cells do not summarise draws rows at every zoom.** `scan.plan()` then has no
task below `Z`, and the level choice picks from the planned matrices only — so a coarse camera draws
vertices, over as many finest tiles as are in view, and the mark budget is not met. That is the owner's
call of 2026-09-29: a filtered picture is rows or nothing, never a cell that counts members the filter
excluded. What keeps it usable is the tileset — the tiles nearest the centre first, the cache, and the
manifest's statistics pruning tiles no row of which can match.

**The cost of drawing is the other half of the argument.** The aggregate branch that grouped by column
joined the edge relation to the vertex relation twice under `DISTINCT` — natively **390.5 s at 1M**,
and in the browser *"Out of Memory Error: could not allocate block of size 256.0 KiB (3.1 GiB/3.1 GiB
used)"*. A cells level reads one level's tiles and joins nothing. **Its lines are the rung's quotient
and nothing else** — the owner's call of 2026-09-29, and the next rule's subject.

**The honest total comes free.** A cell carries its count, so *20,000 marks standing for 1,000,000
vertices* is a sum the view already holds, not a `count(*) OVER ()` that materialised the whole match
before sampling it (inefficiency 5).

**What this does not settle.** The cells pyramid has not been scored with this metric in a browser;
the mipmap row was computed natively over the same corpora, and a cells level cut to a 20,000-mark
budget is a coarser mipmap than the ceiling row.

**What would reverse it:** a cells level scoring worse than the stride did at the same mark budget —
0.167, 0.240, 0.269 — measured the same way. That would mean the writer's cells are the wrong summary,
not that sampling was right.

*Held by* `packages/graph/src/core/tileset.test.ts`, "chooses the finest zoom whose tiles in view fit
the limit"; `packages/graph/src/core/store.test.ts`, "reads the payload at a close camera and a rung
at a far one"; `packages/graph/src/index.test.ts`, "keeps the aggregate far view deleted, names and
all" — the column-grouped summary and its zoom threshold stay deleted.

A cell is not a super-node: it has no vertex identity, takes no reserved vertex type, and fossil's
`node` never returns one. What a click on a cell reaches is the tiles under it at `Z`, one shift away.

## A tile is an address, not a verb

The camera resolves to tiles by **a cull over the tile matrix**, bought once at open and kept. No
request, no query and no service sits between the camera moving and the tiles in view being known.
Questions that return **ids** are a separate surface, and the canvas applies their answers over the
tiles it already holds.

A verb on the drawing path is a round trip on the path whose entire design exists to have none.
fossil's read surface is a closed set of verbs, and its own page has a section headed *What is not a
verb* whose first entry is the camera: `viewport` and `materialize_graph` were verbs and went,
**because the camera is addressed**. Proposing a tile verb here is proposing to re-add `viewport`
under another name.

**The architecture is three lines, and the seam draws the first:**

```text
draw    camera → cull over published boxes → scan.read(address)    memory, then a read per tile
ask     verb → rows → ids                                          the verbs, or SQL, on fossil's side
join    ids → what the canvas draws                                the predicate, below
```

**The tile matrix is the OGC TileMatrixSet's shape**, and it is the corpus's to publish:
`tileMatrix(type)` answers, synchronously, the extent — which is also the coordinate box — and one
matrix per `z`, coarsest first, each with its `kind` (`rows` or `cells`), `count`, `shift`, `tileRows`
and **every tile with its `rows` and `bbox`**. The address is `{ type, z, tile }`, not OGC's row and
column, because a rank axis is one-dimensional; and the boxes are published because a rank's place on
the plane cannot be computed from the matrix — which is also why the view culls rather than computes.

**`scan.plan()` is asked once per filter, never per camera move**: a scan is a type, a filter and a
projection, with no zoom and no box, and its synchronous plan is the tasks at **every** zoom that
survive the manifest's statistics. The camera's cull is intersected with that set in memory.
`scan.read` accepts any address, not only a planned one — which is what lets the view read the tile a
far end falls in, or the tiles under a cell.

**What this closes.** Opening used to read a Parquet footer over every payload file to learn the tile
boxes — 4,892 requests and 40 MB at ten million (inefficiency 8). The writer publishes the boxes in
the manifest — 2,654 entries at ten million, every tile at every `z` — and the view reads them there.

**What it does nothing for is first paint**, and that is worth stating because it is the number a
reader looks at first: the opening view is the whole extent and every tile intersects it. What the
pyramid adds is that the opening view is read at a coarse level, which *is* fewer bytes. Addressing is
for the camera moving.

**What would reverse it:** a level whose tile set cannot be derived from what the view already holds —
if choosing among levels, or among tiles, ever needs an answer from the corpus rather than a number
from the manifest, the camera has become a question and this is wrong.

*Held by* `packages/graph/src/core/store.test.ts`, "plans once per question, never per camera move";
`packages/graph/src/index.test.ts`, "keeps the neighbourhood question deleted, and says what it was
for" — the verb that tempted a neighbourhood query onto the drawing path stays gone.

## An edge is drawn from bytes in hand

At `Z`, an edge is drawn when **both ends have a position among the tiles the tileset holds** — the
cache, not only the tiles in view — and it is keyed by its pair of `VertexId`s, so the copy read from
its source's tile and the copy read from its target's tile are one edge. An edge shorter than **three
screen pixels** is not composed.

**Edges are read per tile, in both directions.** `corpus.edges({ from: address, direction })` answers
a tile's relations out of the half aligned on `direction` — `src` for the source-aligned adjacency,
`dst` for the target-aligned one — as batches of `src` and `dst` ids. The `dst` half is what the old
reader could not ask: an edge whose source was outside the window lived in a tile the window did not
read, and was never drawn (inefficiency 10). Reading both is also what retires the anchor query — the
far end's position came from a `NOT (bbox)` scan over every vertex outside the window (2), and it now
comes from a tile already in the cache or not at all. The far end's tile is addressable — its
`dense_id` over `tileRows` — so reading it is possible; it is not done, because it would turn one
window's read into its whole neighbourhood's.

**Below `Z` the lines are the quotient, and only the quotient.** Where the writer published a rung's
quotient, `edges` answers a cell tile with `src_cell`, `dst_cell` and a `weight`, source-aligned only;
where it did not, a coarse zoom draws no lines. A line between two cells is drawn when both are in the
cache, weighted by its `weight`. That is the owner's call of 2026-09-29, and it is fossil's own
conclusion from the other side: a coarse view draws the field and not the relation.

**The discard moves from SQL to composition.** `longEnough` dropped a stub before it was sent; the
tile's edges now arrive whole, and the renderer drops a stub before it is uploaded. The saving is the
same fraction measured against the million-vertex corpus — a window of 6,431 marks sent 39,072 links,
17,093 of them shorter than the dots at their ends — and it is now a saving in GPU links rather than
in rows.

**A multi-type corpus draws none of its cross-type edges**, and nothing is dropped in silence. A canvas
draws one vertex type, a `dense_id` numbers within a type, and a join across two types' numbering
draws a line between two vertices with no relation at all. `edges` answers that itself: its `declined`
list is fossil's `Gap`s — `other-space` for a relation whose far end is another type, `not-declared`
for an orientation the corpus does not publish — and the view reports them as they come.

**What would reverse it:** a tiling where the vertices just outside a window are not in tiles the
cache keeps — measured as the share of a window's edges whose far end the cache cannot place — at
which point the view reads far-end tiles by address. Or a canvas at a resolution where the median
drawn edge clears three pixels.

The two halves ran as SQL in `anchorCte` and `longEnough`, and left with it. A far end the cache
holds but the view does not is placed past the marks at radius zero, so the edge has an end and the
vertex draws nothing.

*Held by* `packages/graph/src/render/compose.test.ts`, "composes an edge read from both of its ends

## A filter is a predicate, not a mask

The page's crossfilter rides **in the scan that draws**, as scan's typed `filter` — Iceberg's
expression vocabulary spelled as data: `{ column, op, value }` for a comparison, `{ column, op, values }`
for `in` and `not in`, `{ column, op }` for the two null tests, joined by `and`, `or` and `not`. fossil
binds it when the scan is built, so a column no matrix carries fails there — before anything is
planned — and reaches `onFailure`. What comes back is what
survives. The greyout expresses one thing: which points the reader picked.

**What the mask cost, measured** against the million-vertex corpus in Chrome, one window of 20,000
marks:

| the mask, per filter change | |
|---|---|
| `SELECT dense_id FROM corpus_Node` — every surviving id | 377 ms |
| widening a million ids into `(type, dense)` identities | 43 ms |
| looking each one up against the 20,000 resident | 30 ms |

Four hundred and fifty milliseconds on every brush, to shade a picture that never held more than
twenty thousand marks. The predicate costs the opposite: the same window measured **8.4 ms filtered
against 12.0 ms unfiltered**, because fewer rows survive to be read.

**The graph listens to the selection; it is not a client of the coordinator.** A Mosaic client is a
thing the coordinator queries on its behalf: on a selection change, mosaic-core 0.29.2's
`updateSelection` calls `client.query(filter)` and hands the answer to `updateClient` as SQL to run.
The graph has no SQL to hand over, so it takes the part of the protocol that is about *filtering* and
not the part that is about *querying*: it listens for the `Selection`'s `value` event, reads
`selection.predicate(graph)`, translates mosaic-sql's expression nodes — column references, literals,
`BinaryOpNode`, `BetweenOpNode`, `InOpNode`, `AndNode`, `OrNode`, and `NOT` — into scan's `filter`,
and builds a new scan, whose synchronous plan replaces the tileset's candidates and reloads the tiles
while keeping the picture. **A node it cannot translate
is reported through `onFailure`, never dropped**: a dropped clause draws an unfiltered picture that
reads as a filtered one.

**Publishing takes the exemption back.** A crossfilter normally exempts a view from its own clause,
on the reasoning that a view which *fades* an excluded row does not need it. The moment the canvas
draws what survives, that inversion deletes the reader's context: a lasso of thirteen nodes would
answer by removing the other 1,530. So the clause the graph publishes names the graph in its
`clients` set, and `Selection.predicate` skips it for the graph and applies it to every chart.
Verified in the browser: an order selecting 231 contracts left 1,543 nodes drawn with 231 lit; a
clause published by anything else redrew the canvas as 35 points and 0 edges, and retracting it
restored 1,543 and 4,280.

<Callout type="warn">
**Connections queue; they do not overlap.** Two plans costed a pan at roughly forty milliseconds on
the reasoning that three queries on separate connections would collapse to the slowest. Across four
runs, a trivial query on a second connection answered at 511.8, 462.9, 454.8 and 449.5 ms against
sorts of 511.7, 462.8, 454.7 and 449.4. DuckDB-WASM is one worker behind one message port. That is why
the tileset keeps one read in flight and spends its effort on the order of the queue.
</Callout>

**What this does not touch:** the reads about the corpus rather than about the page — the tile matrix,
its counts, the extent — take no filter. A total that shrank with the filters would make the view's
own *20,000 of 1,000,000* a fraction of itself, which is the one number a bounded renderer owes its
reader honestly.

**What would reverse it:** a drawing read that cannot carry an arbitrary predicate — a tile served
from a fixed URL, with no scan behind it — where the surviving ids really are the only thing the two
halves can exchange. Or a crossfilter clause whose predicate is not translatable into scan's
expression shape and is common enough that failing loudly is the wrong answer.

Mosaic queues a `value` event behind one whose listeners have not settled, so a second change of the
crossfilter reaches the graph a microtask after the first; nothing here depends on the two arriving
together.

*Held by* `packages/graph/src/core/filter.test.ts`, against a real `Selection`: "translates the clauses
a chart publishes", "is re-asked when the page filters something", "is exempt from a clause that
names it", "reports a node it cannot translate, and never draws the unfiltered picture as
filtered".

## The renderer uploads what changed, and renders once a frame

cosmos.gl 3.4 takes a whole array per setter — `setPointPositions`, `setPointColors`, `setLinks` and
the rest each replace their buffer, and there is no sub-range upload. So **what changed** is decided at
the granularity cosmos.gl offers: the *kind* of buffer, and the *set* of tiles.

- **A tile is encoded once.** Its `Batch` — fossil's structural columnar shape, `numRows` and
  `getChild(name).toArray()`, with no Arrow dependency on either side — becomes its typed arrays — positions, category ordinals, sizes,
  identities — when it arrives, and again only when a channel binding changes. Composition is a
  concatenation of arrays already made, not a re-encode.
- **Each kind has a dirty flag.** A change of visible tiles dirties positions, links and the per-point
  arrays; a look or a theme change dirties colours, sizes and shapes and nothing else; an `update` that
  selects the same visible set dirties nothing, and nothing is uploaded.
- **One frame renders once.** Every flag is discharged in one `requestAnimationFrame` that calls
  `render()` a single time.

What this closes is inefficiency 9: today a new answer uploads positions and links and renders in one
effect, while the look's effect uploads the per-point arrays and renders again in the same commit — two
`graph.update()` passes, adjacency lists included, for one change. It also retires the reason the host
bridged the overlays to the renderer with a ref: the frame that renders is the frame that repaints the
labels.

`Resident` — the map from identity to buffer index — is rebuilt per composition, which is what it
already was per answer. Everything that outlives one composition is still held as a `VertexId`.

**What would reverse it:** cosmos.gl gaining a sub-range upload, at which point the unit of change
becomes a tile's range and composition stops copying. Or a measurement where concatenating cached
arrays costs more than re-encoding from rows — which would mean the cache is holding the wrong thing.

*Held by* `packages/graph/src/render/renderer.test.ts`, "uploads each buffer once for a change of
tiles, and renders once", "repaints colours, sizes and shapes on a look change, and uploads no
geometry", "uploads nothing when an update selects the same visible set";
`packages/graph/src/render/compose.test.ts`, "composes nothing new when the visible set has not
changed"; `packages/graph/src/render/when-ready.test.ts`, "has no module calling a setter on an
instance it has not waited for"; `packages/graph/src/react/graph-root.test.tsx`, "does not rebuild
the renderer because a callback changed identity".

## The React API is a store and an adapter

The graph's state changes outside React — tiles arrive, the camera moves, a filter lands — at frame
rate. So the core owns it as a **store** with TanStack Query's observer shape, and React subscribes:

- **The core** is `createGraph(options)`: `subscribe(listener)`, `getSnapshot()`, `setOptions(options)`
  and `destroy()`. It holds the tileset, the channels, the selection, the focus and the motion, and it
  knows nothing about React. That is `QueryObserver` in `@tanstack/query-core`, and the reason is the
  same: the thing observed lives longer than any component.
- **The adapter** is `useGraph(props)`, and it is `useBaseQuery`'s three moves: create the store once
  in `useState`, subscribe with `useSyncExternalStore` over `subscribe` and `getSnapshot`, and hand it
  the latest props with `setOptions` in an effect.
- **The surface is Ark's.** `useGraph` **creates** the api, `GraphRoot` is `useGraph` plus the
  context, `GraphRootProvider` takes an api a host built itself, and `useGraphContext` **reads** it —
  `useDialog`, `DialogRoot`, `DialogRootProvider` and `useDialogContext` in `@ark-ui/react`, one for
  one.

**Where it departs.** Zag's React adapter keeps a machine's state in React state through `useBindable`
and does not use `useSyncExternalStore`; TanStack Table v8 does the same through `useState` and
`setOptions` on every render. Both are right for state a user's gesture moves. A tile arriving is not
a gesture, and a store that React re-derives on each render would re-run composition on renders that
changed nothing. And an Ark api's value is its prop getters, which distribute props across many parts —
there are parts now, but each renders one element the context fully describes, so there is still no
`getRootProps()`, and inventing one for the resemblance would be cargo cult.

**What would reverse it:** a second framework adapter never arriving *and* the store costing more than
it buys — measured as renders per camera move with and without `useSyncExternalStore`. The core/adapter
split is paid for by the frame-rate state even with one adapter.

**Where it departs from `useBaseQuery`, in one line.** TanStack calls `setOptions` on every render and
compares inside; here the effect's dependencies are the props themselves, and the callbacks —
`onFailure`, `onSelect`, `onFocus` — are read through a ref, so a host's inline callback is never a
change of options. The store also starts listening to the crossfilter on its first subscriber and
lets the tiles go on its last, `QueryObserver`'s `onSubscribe` and `onUnsubscribe`, which is what
survives StrictMode mounting everything twice.

*Held by* `packages/graph/src/core/store.test.ts`, "notifies subscribers once when a tile loads",
"keeps getSnapshot stable between notifications", "lets the tiles go when the last subscriber
leaves"; `packages/graph/src/react/graph-root.test.tsx`, "does not reach setOptions on a render that
changed no prop"; `packages/graph/src/index.test.ts`, "is Ark's four: useGraph creates, GraphRoot
provides, GraphRootProvider takes, useGraphContext reads".

## The coordinate box is the corpus's extent, and this side declares no default

`@kanzo-tech/graph` declares no `SPACE`. The renderer is constructed with no `spaceSize`, and the
framing sets it from the larger side of `tileMatrix(type)`'s extent before the first tile is read. The
coordinate box is owned by whatever wrote the positions.

`SPACE = 4096` was passed to cosmos.gl and **hand-copied into both bench generators**, which is why the
bench never disagreed with it. A corpus fossil writes does not stay inside it: over the million-vertex
corpus the extent is x ∈ [−345, 645396], y ∈ [−188, 516293] — **157× the box the renderer was
announcing.**

**Nothing visible was broken by the wrong box**, and how nothing reported it is the useful half.
`spaceSize` enters every cosmos.gl 3.4.0 render path as a **pure translation**: the vertex shader's
`2·point/S − 1` is immediately multiplied by `S/screenSize`, leaving `(2·point − S)/screenSize`. There
is no clipping to the box, and the camera is fitted from the extent anyway. **The reason to fix it is
therefore not a defect:** the box was a false statement about someone else's data.

**Two cases do not fit, both measured at 800×600.** cosmos.gl's d3-zoom carries
`scaleExtent([1e-3, ∞])`, and framing an extent needs `screen / span`: the million fits at 0.00116, but
**the five-million corpus does not** — the fit wants 0.000151, the zoom clamps at 0.001, and about 15%
of the width is on screen with no way to ask for the rest. Nothing here moves that floor. And the
renderer reduces the box to half the device's `maxTextureDimension2D` whenever it meets that limit —
a translation like every other space size, left visible in the console rather than clamped on our side.

**What would reverse it:** a corpus with no extent to publish, or cosmos.gl making `spaceSize`
init-only, or a render path where the box stops being a translation — a live simulation reads it as its
gravity centre, and no measurement of that exists yet.

*Held by* `packages/graph/src/coordinate-box.test.ts`, "declares no coordinate-space constant of its
own"; `packages/graph/src/render/renderer.test.ts`, "frames the corpus's extent before it reads
anything, and only once". The renderer is built with `rescalePositions: false`: cosmos.gl otherwise
rescales positions whose span fits inside `spaceSize` when no simulation runs, and a composition of a
few tiles always does.

## A dense id is thirty-two bits

`VertexId` packs `(type_idx, dense_id)` as `type << 32 | dense`, so neither half may exceed 2³²−1.
`denseOf` and `typeOf` return `number`, which holds 32 bits **exactly**. It is what the writer emits —
`Node.vertex.yml` declares the column as an unsigned word of exactly this width — so the ceiling is an
agreement with fossil rather than an assumption we made and forgot.

**The gap this exists to name.** The corpus's own page is *wider than we are*: a `dense_id` is "an
unsigned integer that may carry more than 53 bits", with border vectors at 2³¹ and 2⁵³. Today that is
not a conflict, because the writer emits `uint32`. It becomes one the moment fossil widens the column,
and the symptom would be silent: a dense value above 2³² overflows into the type field, vertex
`(0, 2³²)` and vertex `(1, 0)` become one identity, and nothing raises.

Recorded rather than fixed, because widening costs what the current packing buys: a 40/24 or 48/16
split puts one half past 2⁵³ in the general case, and every accessor then hands back a `bigint`.
**What would make it cheap to change:** one place. `TYPE_SHIFT` and `DENSE_MASK` are the whole of the
packing, and nothing else in the package looks inside a `VertexId`.

**What it does not touch: the `bigint` decision.** A `VertexId` is a `bigint` because a 64-bit value
crossing into JavaScript is the failure that recurs across every multi-language format surveyed —
`mapbox/node-s2` is a *binding* and still returned wrong cell ids.

**What would reverse it:** a corpus whose manifest declares a `dense_id` wider than the packing holds, or
a vertex type count past the same bound.

*Held by* `packages/graph/src/core/resident.ts`, `TYPE_SHIFT` / `DENSE_MASK`;
`packages/graph/src/core/resident.test.ts`, "round-trips the far corner of the whole 64-bit range,
which a float64 could not".

## A look is form, and a channel is a binding

`Look` is `form`, and carries no encoding. What a channel carries is said by **binding it on the
root**, under Plot's names and Plot's rule that a CSS colour is a **constant** and a column name is a
**channel**: `fill`, `symbol`, `r`, `stroke` and `title`. `symbol` is a channel in its own right beside `fill`, so
identity may be drawn as colour, as shape, or as both. `stroke` absent leaves each link tinted by the
vertex it leaves; a constant makes links plain structure.

**A binding is a projection.** `fill`, `r` and `title` as columns become the scan's `select`, so
changing one makes a new scan over the same tiles — which reloads them while keeping the picture, and
restarts nothing. `fill` as a constant and `stroke` never reach a scan, and `symbol` reaches it only as
the one categorical column when `fill` is a constant: otherwise shape reads the column `fill` already
projected, and a link's tint is a decision about drawing. `isColour` is the
constant-versus-column test, narrowed to `var(…)`, a hex and the colour functions, so **a bare word is
always a column**.

A theme may not rebind an encoding, and a prop named `fill` that paints nothing is a lie the caller
cannot see through: under the look named `Ink` the `fill` binding once painted nothing, and the mismatch
reached the wire contract. Vega-Lite is explicit about the general case — `config` sets defaults for
marks, scales, axes and legends and may not touch `encoding`.

**The case that does not fit.** `Ink`'s radius floor exists **because** it spends shape on identity and
size on degree at once. So splitting form from encoding leaves that obligation graded against the
composition — and it catches a host pairing `symbol` with a dense form's ramp, which nothing
previously reported.

**What this does not touch.** A tile still carries **one** categorical column; binding `fill` and
`symbol` to two different columns would need a second everywhere and is not paid for. `x` and `y` are
not channels — in a laid-out corpus a position is a fact and the index every spatial question is asked
against. And nothing about colour *values*: the graph reads the page's categorical scale and does not
author a colour.

**What would reverse it:** the composition check never firing. If every host that binds `symbol` also
asks for the form that was bundled with it, the pairing was the abstraction.

*Held by* `packages/graph/src/render/graph-looks.ts`, `Look`; `packages/graph/src/core/channels.ts`,
`Channels`, `projectionOf`; `packages/graph/src/render/obligations.ts`, `gradeComposition`;
`packages/graph/src/core/store.test.ts`, "projects the bound columns and nothing a constant names".

## What the view takes from the corpus contract, and what is still open

The backend half is fossil's contract as written, and it answers most of what this page needs in its
own words: a synchronous `tileMatrix` that lists every tile with its rows and box and has no empty
tile; a synchronous `plan()` over every zoom, once per filter; `read` of any `{ type, z, tile }`; a
finest tile of at most 4,096 rows; a cell row's centroid, `count`, `internal`, `mode` and `purity`;
quotient edges below `Z`; `edges` declining with fossil's `GapReason`; `node(id)` with a position; and
an `Engine.query` that interrupts the running statement on the signal.

What is still open, each fossil's to answer or the owner's to rule on:

- **Names for a channel's ordinals.** `channels:` gives a domain, `0` to `domain − 1`, and no labels,
  so a legend over the one host's `kind` can say *3* but not what 3 is. Either the manifest carries the
  labels, or the host passes them, and passing them is data rather than policy.
- **The in-edges of a cell.** The quotient is source-aligned only, so a coarse tile's lines are the
  ones leaving it; a line arriving from a cell whose tile is not cached is not drawn. That is the same
  bytes-in-hand rule the finest level follows, and it is named here because at a coarse zoom the cache
  is most of the picture.
- **The cell columns by role.** fossil's barrel exports the payload's role table and not the cells':
  `cell_id`, `count` and `mode` are spelled in `render/encode.ts`, the one place this package writes a
  writer's column name down. `CELL_ADDRESS`, `CELL_TALLY` and `CELL_MODE` exist in fossil's generated
  vocabulary; exporting them is fossil's call.
- **The cost of a filtered coarse view.** Rows at every zoom means a whole-extent camera over a
  filtered corpus reads every finest tile the statistics cannot prune — 245 at a million, 2,442 at ten
  million. That is accepted, and not yet measured.
